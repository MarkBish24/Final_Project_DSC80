US Power Grid Resilience

A Deep Dive into Major Power Outages (2000–2016)

Step 1: Introduction

This project investigates the historical patterns and predictive possibilities of major power outages in the United States. Utilizing a comprehensive dataset of outages recorded between January 2000 and July 2016, we analyze how geographical, environmental, and infrastructural factors correlate with grid failure severity.

The Core Objective: Can we predict the duration of an outage as soon as it begins, allowing for better emergency resource allocation and public safety warnings?

1,534 Observations
57 Variables
16 Years Time Span

Step 2: Data Cleaning & EDA

Cleaning Methodology

  • Header Normalization: Removed bureaucratic metadata rows to isolate raw data fields.
  • Temporal Synthesis: Combined disparate Year, Month, Day, and Time columns into unified OUTAGE.START and OUTAGE.RESTORATION datetime objects.
  • Metric Conversion: Standardized total outage length into Outage_Duration_Minutes for high-precision regression.
  • Validation: Enforced chronological consistency (restoration must occur after start).

Mapping the Outage Landscape

Our initial exploration revealed significant regional disparities. California leads the nation in total outage count, likely due to a combination of dense population and environmental risks (wildfires/wind).

However, when looking at average duration, the story shifts. While California has the most outages, states like New York and Texas frequently face longer recovery times per event.

Step 3: Assessment of Missingness

NMAR, MAR, and MCAR

We analyzed columns with significant missing values, specifically Demand_Loss_MW and Customers_Affected.

  • MAR Discovery: We found that Demand_Loss_MW missingness is significantly correlated with the Year. This implies that reporting standards for precise megawatt loss improved over time, suggesting the data is Missing at Random based on the date.
  • MAR Discovery: Customers_Affected missingness was found to be dependent on the State, likely due to varying utility reporting requirements between different state legislatures.

Step 4: Hypothesis Testing

We conducted a Permutation Test to determine if geographical location (State) significantly influences outage duration.

Null Hypothesis (H₀): Outage duration is independent of state. Differences are due to random noise.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): Outage duration differs significantly across states.

0.015 Empirical p-Value

Conclusion: With a p-value of 0.015, we reject the null hypothesis at the 5% level. There is strong evidence that regional infrastructure and local climate conditions create state-specific "outage signatures."

Step 5: Framing a Prediction Problem

We aim to solve a Regression Problem: Predicting Outage_Duration_Minutes.

Why? Operational readiness. If a utility knows a storm will cause a 10-hour outage versus a 2-hour one, they can prioritize repair crews and alert medical facilities more effectively.

Metrics:

  • MAE (Mean Absolute Error): Chosen for its direct interpretability in minutes of error.
  • RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error): Chosen to penalize large, catastrophic misses.

Step 6: Baseline Model

Our baseline used a simple Linear Regression pipeline. We selected four basic features present at the moment an outage is detected:

  • Quantitative: Anomaly_Level, Total_Sales
  • Categorical: Climate_Region, Climate_Category

Baseline Performance: The model struggled with complex interactions, yielding a Test MAE of approximately 3,081 minutes.

Step 7: Final Model

Advancing to Random Forest

To capture the non-linear realities of power grids, we implemented a Random Forest Regressor with customized feature engineering:

  • Season Encoding: Binarized months to group extreme weather seasons (Summer/Winter vs. Shoulder seasons).
  • Skewness Correction: Applied QuantileTransformer (Normal distribution output) to population density and sales figures.
  • Hyperparameter Tuning: Used 3-fold GridSearchCV to optimize tree depth and split criteria.

Performance Gain: The final model reached a Test MAE of 2,137 minutes—a ~30% improvement in accuracy over the baseline.

Residual Analysis

The log-log residual plot reveals that while the model handles medium-scale outages well, the extreme 10,000+ minute outliers remain difficult to point-predict, reflecting the chaotic nature of massive grid failures.

Step 8: Fairness Analysis

Does the model perform worse during extreme climate events? We compared MAE for outages occurring during "Severe Weather" (Anomaly > 0.5) vs. "Normal Weather".

  • Group X: Severe Weather Anomaly
  • Group Y: Under-threshold Anomaly
  • Null Hypothesis: The model is fair; MAE is the same across both groups.
0.365 Fairness p-Value

Conclusion: We fail to reject the null hypothesis. The model appears fair and maintains consistent accuracy regardless of the severity of the climate anomaly.
