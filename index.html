US Power Grid Resilience

A Deep Dive into Major Power Outages (2000–2016)

Step 1: Introduction

Major power outages are more than just an inconvenience; they are critical events that impact public safety and economic stability. This project investigates a historical record of major power outages in the United States to uncover patterns of risk.

The Central Research Question: Can we accurately predict the Duration of a Power Outage using regional climate data, infrastructure metrics, and the timing of the event?

Understanding the expected duration of an outage at its onset allows utility companies and emergency services to coordinate repairs and communicate with the public with greater precision. For residents, it means the difference between waiting out a storm and needing immediate relocation for safety.

Dataset Overview

The dataset analyzed here contains 1,534 major outage events recorded across the United States between January 2000 and July 2016. Each observation includes geographical details, climate anomalies, and local electricity consumption metrics.

Variable Description
Outage_Duration_Minutes The total time elapsed (in minutes) from the reported start of the outage event until power was restored to all affected customers.
Customers_Affected The reported number of customers who lost power during the major outage event.
Anomaly_Level Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) estimated as a 3-month running mean of ERSST.v4 SST anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region (5°N to 5°S, 120–170°W).
Total_Sales Total electricity consumption in the specific U.S. State (measured in megawatt-hours).
Climate_Region Classification of the continental U.S. into nine climatically consistent regions as specified by the National Centers for Environmental Information.
Climate_Category Climate episodes ("Warm", "Cold", or "Normal") defined by a threshold of ± 0.5 °C for the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI).
Percent_Urban_Population Percentage of the total state population represented by the urban population (measured in %).
U.S._State The geographical state in the continental U.S. where the outage event was localized.
Season An engineered feature grouping the outage start into meteorological seasonal clusters based on the reporting month.

Step 2: Data Cleaning & EDA

Data Cleaning & Preprocessing

The raw dataset comes from official regulatory incident reports filed by utility companies. Because these forms are collected across different states and power companies, the raw data is quite messy and full of inconsistencies. To turn it into clean, model-ready data, we built a preprocessing pipeline with the following steps:

  • Cleaning Up the Headers: The original spreadsheet came with several rows of government metadata and titles at the very top. We stripped these out to isolate the actual data fields and renamed the titles in a way to make it easier to understand.
  • Combining Date and Time Pieces: The dataset originally split timestamps across separate columns for the Year, Month, Day, and specific times. We stitched these pieces back together to create two unified, high-precision datetime columns: OUTAGE.START and OUTAGE.RESTORATION.
  • Calculating Outage Durations: Using our newly combined timestamps, we calculated the exact number of minutes between when an outage started and when it ended, creating our main target variable: Outage_Duration_Minutes. Standardizing this into minutes gives us a precise, continuous number that is perfect for running regression models.
  • Filtering Out Chronological Errors: Real-world data entry forms are prone to human error. We ran a validation check to make sure that a power grid's restoration time didn't accidentally happen *before* its start time. Any glitchy rows with negative durations or impossible timelines were filtered out to ensure our model isn't learning from bad data.
  • Handling Missing Data (NaNs): To ensure a robust dataset for modeling, we implemented a structured imputation strategy:
    • Dropping Irrelevant Features: We dropped Hurricane_Names as the missingness was "by design" (only relevant for a small subset of events).
    • Indicator Variables: Created binary tags (_missing) for Customers_Affected and Demand_Loss_MW to preserve the signal that a value was originally absent before imputation.
    • Median Imputation: For major numerical columns including Customers_Affected, Demand_Loss_MW, and electricity price/sales metrics, we filled missing values with the median to maintain central tendency without being skewed by outliers.
    • Categorical "Unknown" Labels: For Cause_Category_Detail, Climate_Category, and Climate_Region, we replaced NaNs with an "Unknown" category to maintain the data's multi-class structure.
    • Listwise Deletion for Target: We converted Outage_Duration_Minutes to numeric and dropped rows where this value remained missing, as we cannot train or validate a regression model on unknown targets.

Step 3: Assessment of Missingness

NMAR, MAR, and MCAR

We analyzed columns with significant missing values, specifically Demand_Loss_MW and Customers_Affected.

  • MAR Discovery: We found that Demand_Loss_MW missingness is significantly correlated with the Year. This implies that reporting standards for precise megawatt loss improved over time, suggesting the data is Missing at Random based on the date.
  • MAR Discovery: Customers_Affected missingness was found to be dependent on the State, likely due to varying utility reporting requirements between different state legislatures.

Missingness Permutation Tests

To validate our MAR assumptions, we performed permutation tests on the distributions of observed versus missing data across temporal and geographical variables.

Step 4: Hypothesis Testing

We conducted a Permutation Test to determine whether geographical location (U.S. State) has a statistically significant effect on outage duration.

Null Hypothesis (H₀): Outage duration is independent of state. Any observed differences in average outage duration across states are due to random chance.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): Outage duration differs systematically across states; at least one state has a meaningfully different mean outage duration.

Test Statistic & Significance Level

Our test statistic is the sum of absolute deviations of each state's mean outage duration from the overall global mean. This is a natural choice for a multi-group comparison because it captures the aggregate dispersion of group means without requiring pairwise testing across all 49 states — it increases whenever any state's mean drifts further from the center, making it sensitive to the alternative hypothesis we care about.

We chose a significance level of α = 0.05, which is the standard threshold in data science and gives us a 5% false-positive rate — an appropriate balance between being too permissive and too conservative for an exploratory analysis of this kind.

84,990 Observed Test Statistic
0.015 Empirical p-Value
0.05 Significance Level (α)

Conclusion: With a p-value of 0.015 — below our threshold of 0.05 — we reject the null hypothesis. The data provides statistically significant evidence that outage durations vary across U.S. states. This suggests, but does not prove, that state-level factors such as regional infrastructure age, local climate conditions, and utility regulatory environments play a role in determining how long outages last.

Step 5: Framing a Prediction Problem

We frame this as a Regression Problem: predicting Outage_Duration_Minutes — the total number of minutes an outage lasts from its reported start until full power restoration.

Why This Response Variable?

Outage duration is the most operationally actionable metric for utilities and emergency responders. If a utility company can estimate at an outage's onset whether it will last 2 hours or 20 hours, they can make better decisions about deploying repair crews, alerting hospitals, and communicating with the public. This is a more useful prediction target than, for example, the number of customers affected, which is largely a function of where an outage happens rather than how hard it is to fix.

What Information Is Known at the Time of Prediction?

A key constraint is that our model must only use information that would be observable at the moment an outage is first reported — before we know how long it will last. This means we can use:

  • When the outage starts (Year, Month — used for seasonality)
  • Where it occurs (State, Climate Region, NERC Region)
  • Climate context at the time of the event (Anomaly Level, Climate Category)
  • Infrastructure profile of the region (Total Sales, Rural Population Density, Residential Sales)
  • The reported cause (Cause Category Detail — this is typically logged when the outage is first dispatched)

We explicitly excluded features like Demand_Loss_MW and the final Customers_Affected count from model training features where they would constitute leakage, since accurate figures for those are often only known after restoration is complete.

Evaluation Metrics

  • MAE (Mean Absolute Error): Our primary metric. It gives us the average error in interpretable minutes (e.g., "off by 2,100 minutes on average"). We prefer MAE over R² because R² measures proportion of variance explained and can be misleadingly low when the target has extreme outliers — which ours does. An R² of 0.3 on a heavily right-skewed distribution may still represent a highly useful model in practice.
  • RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error): Used as a secondary metric because it penalizes large individual errors more heavily than MAE. Since catastrophically wrong predictions (e.g., predicting 500 minutes when the actual is 40,000) are especially costly in an emergency-response context, RMSE helps us track those tail failures.

Step 6: Baseline Model

Our baseline model utilizes a Linear Regression pipeline to establish a performance floor. We selected four fundamental features that are typically known at the immediate onset of an outage:

  • Quantitative (2 features): Anomaly_Level, Total_Sales — passed through as-is with no transformation.
  • Nominal (2 features): Climate_Region, Climate_Category — encoded using OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"), which converts each unique category into its own binary column. This prevents the model from assuming any ordinal relationship between climate labels.
  • Ordinal features: None. None of the selected features represent a naturally ordered ranking, so no ordinal encoding was needed.

All preprocessing and model steps were implemented inside a single sklearn Pipeline using a ColumnTransformer to apply the appropriate encoding to each feature type.

Reasoning: These features were chosen because they provide a high-level snapshot of the environment (climate) and the size of the grid (total sales) without requiring complex post-event data. From a data-generating perspective, we expect larger grids and extreme climate regions to have longer "queue" times for repair crews, justifying their inclusion in a basic predictive model.

Performance (Train vs. Test): The model achieved a Train RMSE of 5,254 minutes and a Test RMSE of 7,735 minutes, with a Test MAE of approximately 3,081 minutes. The gap between train and test RMSE indicates the model is not overfitting but is underfitting — it lacks the complexity to capture the non-linear patterns in the data. We do not consider this a "good" model; it serves purely as a baseline to measure improvement against.

Step 7: Final Model

Advancing to Random Forest

While the baseline linear model provided a starting point, it failed to account for the complex, non-linear interactions inherent in power grid data. To address this, we transitioned to a Random Forest Regressor. This algorithm is particularly well-suited for this task because it handles both categorical and numerical data effectively and can capture high-order interactions between features without requiring manual interaction terms.

Feature Engineering & Selection

Beyond the baseline features, we incorporated additional variables and transformations chosen based on their relevance to the data generating process:

  • Cause Category Detail: Categorizing the specific reason for an outage (vandalism vs. thunderstorm) is critical because the repair logistics vary wildly between human-caused and nature-caused events.
  • Season Tracking (Month Binarization): We binarized the Month variable to isolate extreme weather periods (Summer and Winter). This recognizes that outages during peak seasons are often systemic (driven by load or broad storms) whereas shoulder-season outages are often isolated or maintenance-related.
  • Anomaly Binarization: By taking the absolute value of the Anomaly_Level and binarizing it, we created a feature that flags "Extreme Climate States," recognizing that both severe heat (positive anomaly) and severe cold (negative anomaly) place similar structural stress on the grid.
  • Skewness Correction (Quantile Transformer): Features like Rural_Population_Density and Total_Sales were extremely right-skewed. We applied a QuantileTransformer to map these to a normal distribution. In the real world, the impact of a population density increase on repair speed isn't linear; normalising these features allows the model to treat variance in lower and higher ranges with equal priority.

Hyperparameter Tuning

We used 3-fold Cross-Validation via GridSearchCV to optimize our model. Our search explored variations in tree depth, forest size, and splitting criteria. The optimal configuration discovered was:

  • n_estimators: 100
  • max_depth: 10
  • min_samples_split: 2

Performance Improvement: The Final Model achieved a Test MAE of approximately 2,137 minutes. Compared to the Baseline Model's MAE of 3,081 minutes, this represents a ~30% reduction in error. This improvement confirms that the inclusion of non-linear tree-based logic and transformed population metrics significantly enhanced the model's ability to navigate the complex "outage signatures" present in different climate regions.

Step 8: Fairness Analysis

Does our model perform worse for outages that occur during extreme climate conditions? We split the test set into two groups based on the absolute value of the Anomaly_Level:

  • Group X (Extreme Climate): Outages where |Anomaly_Level| > 0.5 — i.e., outages occurring during a significant El Niño or La Niña period.
  • Group Y (Near-Normal Climate): Outages where |Anomaly_Level| ≤ 0.5 — i.e., outages during climatically stable periods.

Hypotheses

Null Hypothesis (H₀): Our model is fair. Its MAE for outages in extreme climate conditions and for outages in near-normal conditions are roughly the same, and any observed difference is due to random chance.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): Our model is unfair. Its MAE for outages in extreme climate conditions is higher than its MAE for outages in near-normal conditions.

Test Statistic & Significance Level

Our test statistic is the difference in MAE between Group X and Group Y (MAEExtreme − MAENormal). A positive value means the model is less accurate during extreme climate events. We used a significance level of α = 0.05 and ran a one-sided permutation test by repeatedly shuffling the group labels and recomputing the difference, generating a null distribution of what the MAE gap would look like by chance alone.

194.9 min Observed MAE Difference
0.365 p-Value
0.05 Significance Level (α)

Conclusion: With a p-value of 0.365 — well above our threshold of 0.05 — we fail to reject the null hypothesis. The observed MAE difference of ~195 minutes between the two groups is well within the range that could arise from random chance alone. This suggests our model does not systematically underperform for outages occurring during extreme climate anomalies, and appears to treat both groups with roughly equivalent accuracy.
